By the age of eleven, a large share of those children can understand their family language far better than they can speak it, and they can speak it far better than they can write it. Teachers call this a [[fluency gap|brecha de fluidez]]. It is not laziness and it is not a lack of love for the language. It is what happens when a child spends six hours a day reading and writing in English and twenty minutes at the table answering a grandmother in Spanish. A smaller number have begun to treat the home language as a resource in the classroom itself: a child who can explain a science idea in Turkish is asked to do so, and then to find the English for it. The schools that do most tend to be the ones that already have a bilingual adult on the staff.<br/><br/>What the research does show, and here the briefing is firmer, is a pattern that holds across languages. Children who keep a strong home language do not fall behind in English. They do not fall behind in mathematics. A language that lives only in the mouth of the family becomes a private code. That can feel like love. It can also become a wall.
