3:Tcc9,The most expensive sentence most of us will write this year will not be in a contract. It will be in an email we sent too fast. A Bangalore HR manager, who asked not to be named, keeps a folder of them. Not to shame anyone: to teach the repair.<br/><br/>The pattern is almost always the same. Somebody is tired. Somebody has a legitimate point — a rota, a delay, a tone in a previous message. They write the point. Then, because they are tired, they write a second sentence that does the damage. In hindsight, they say, they ought to have stopped after the first. They did not. They pressed send.<br/><br/>What happens next is where [[manners|los modales]] and method part company. A lot of people send a second email that begins I'm sorry if you were offended. The HR manager files those under unfinished. They do not name the damage, and they hand the problem to the reader. The emails that actually repair something have four moves, and they are boring on purpose. You name the wording. You take responsibility. You say what you will do differently — a second draft, a night's sleep, getting a colleague to read it. You ask the other person whether they will accept the apology, and you stop.<br/><br/>She is not sentimental about this. "I must admit I have sent the bad kind," she says. "If I had waited, I would not have had a folder. The folder is my second draft, in a way." The office she works in is not British and not American. English is the language in which people who do not share a first language do their jobs. That makes the repair more, not less, important. There is no shared childhood to absorb a harsh line. There is only the sentence.<br/><br/>A social convention in that office, which she would like to export, is that anyone can ask a colleague to read a difficult email before it goes. "I got him to help" is not a confession of weakness. It is how the damage is avoided. She has had the wording of her own messages checked more times than she can count. The people who will not ask are the people who later write I didn't mean it like that, which is true and which is also too late.<br/><br/>None of this is a moral. It is a process. If you have already sent the sentence, the process still runs: name, own, change, ask. If you have not, the process is shorter: wait. She has watched people skip the wait because they wanted the feeling of having been honest. Honesty without a second look is just speed. The folder on her desktop is called, without irony, Sent. She opens it when a new graduate says that apologies are just manners. "They are manners," she says. "They are also the only tool we have when the sentence has already left the building."<br/><br/>A last note, because graduates always ask it: do you write the repair the same day? She says no, unless the damage is still spreading. A night's sleep is part of the method. If she had answered every angry inbox on the afternoon it arrived, the folder would be twice as thick. Even so, she does not recommend silence. A short line — I have read this; I will reply tomorrow — keeps the other person from inventing a worse sentence in the gap. Then you do the four steps, and you stop.
