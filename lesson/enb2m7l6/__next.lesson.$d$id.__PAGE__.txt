It is a finding from a sample of two hundred independent cafés in England and Wales, and the number is significantly higher than most owners guess when they are asked to make a decision with a pad and a pen.<br/><br/>The briefing splits what goes in the bin into three piles, namely: leftovers from plates, food that has gone off, and food that has passed a [[best-before|consumo preferente]] date and been thrown away out of fear. The third pile is the one that surprises people. Best before is not [[use by|fecha de caducidad]]. Use by is a safety date; after it, you do not serve the milk. Best before is a quality date; after it, the yoghurt is often still yoghurt. A generous portion of the waste in the sample was the third kind. Staff had not looked carefully at the label. They had put the whole tray in the bin because the date had turned red on the sticker, and the sticker did not say which kind of date it was.<br/><br/>Year-round availability makes the pile worse, not better. Strawberries in December travel. They arrive looking like strawberries and tasting like a rumour of strawberries, and they go off faster because they have already had a long week. Food miles are not a slogan here; they are a number on a lorry, and the lorry is why the fruit does not last until Sunday. Cafés that source locally on two mornings a week throw less away, not because they are more virtuous, but because the produce has had less time to die in a crate.<br/><br/>The report is careful with recommendations. It does not tell anyone to go plant-based. It does say that batch cooking on a Monday, with a freezer that does not take up the whole kitchen, turns leftovers into Tuesday's soup. It freezes well, the soup, if you do not put cream in it until the bowl. A diet of specials that change every day looks lively on a board and fills the bin on a Thursday. A shorter menu, seasonal, with one plant-based dish that is not bland, fills plates instead.<br/><br/>None of this is new to a cook. What is new is the permission to write it down as a process, not as a feeling. The cafés in the sample that kept a notebook by the bin for four weeks cut their waste to a great extent — not to zero, and not by magic. They made an effort with the dates, they had a look at the portions, and they stopped ordering the fruit that looked honest in the catalogue and arrived tired. The bin, which has no opinions, already knew.<br/><br/>A last figure, because owners ask for one. Cafés that cut one special and one imported fruit from the board saved, on average, the price of a week's milk. That is not a fortune. It is also not nothing, and it is the kind of finding that survives a second count. Significantly higher waste tracked with year-round boards, not with busy Saturdays. Busy Saturdays filled plates. Tired fruit filled bins. The distinction is the whole briefing, and it fits in a notebook. Owners who want a single slogan will not find one. The briefing's last line is a process, not a virtue: look at the label, look at the portion, look at the crate, write it down.
