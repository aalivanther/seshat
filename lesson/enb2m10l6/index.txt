17:Tb74,This exhibition began, as most of ours do, with a gap in the collection. We have still lifes; we have a small holding of contemporary photography; we do not have, or had not had until this spring, a single work that held those two traditions in the same frame. The photograph in the small room at the back, on loan from a municipal gallery in Leeds, is that work. It shows a woman on a station platform. The caption gives her no name. The lighting is the lighting of a winter afternoon in the North, which is to say it is not kind.<br/><br/>Visitors who have been walking around the main rooms first — the still lifes on the west wall, the installation of stacked chairs in the centre — sometimes ask why we have given the quietest picture the quietest room. The answer is practical and it is also the argument of the show. The installation is loud by design: it is what had been going on in this neighbourhood in the years when the gallery was closed, the years of chairs left on pavements and of rooms that no longer had a use. The still lifes, most of them from the collection, are what the neighbourhood looks like when nobody is performing it. The photograph is what happens when those two facts occupy the same person.<br/><br/>I had been looking for a loan of this kind for three years. Leeds said no, then said not yet, then said yes on the condition that we hung it alone. That condition, which I had been ready to refuse, proved to be the right one. Standing in the doorway of the small room, you cannot take a picture of the picture. You have to stay.<br/><br/>A note on the written word, because several visitors have asked. I had done the research — dates, printers, the name of the station, the likely year of the woman's coat — and I left most of it in the catalogue.
